## Executive frame

The system has rotated rather than normalised. Physical energy scarcity and inflation pressure strengthened sharply, but the US long end rallied from its September extreme. That combination shifts the binding constraint from a single duration shock to a more difficult policy-and-margin squeeze: oil and European gas are acute, inflation is re-accelerating, the Fed and RBA have little room to accommodate, while real yields remain high enough to pressure long-duration assets.

The second shift is within equities. Headline US indices weakened, yet equal-weight and smaller-company breadth did not confirm a full liquidation. The active fracture is narrower: AI capital intensity and free-cash-flow conversion are being repriced. This is a concentration and financing-quality problem, not yet a system-wide credit event.

China remains the global demand weak link. October manufacturing PMI fell back below 50, while lithium, nickel and iron ore weakened despite acute energy inflation. Copper held its ground, separating electrification and supply-quality demand from broad Chinese-cycle demand. Gold’s October fall despite lower nominal and real yields also broke the clean defensive relationship; positioning, dollar firmness and liquidity now matter alongside rates.

**Proposed governing regime:** **SHIFTING — acute physical-energy and multi-central-bank stagflation is intact; duration disorder has eased below escalation; AI cash-flow/concentration fracture is active; broad disorderly risk-off is not fully restored.**

## Part 1 — System map: what moves what

### 1. Rates, dollar and policy

- US 10-year nominal and real yields fell roughly 30bp during October, but finished near 5.0% and 2.6% respectively—still restrictive, not benign.
- The Fed held at 3.75–4.00% on 28 October, with two dissents favouring a hike. The next decision is 9 December; the key market variable is whether inflation forces a renewed tightening path.
- Australia’s September CPI rose 4.3% y/y and trimmed-mean inflation 3.6% y/y. The RBA’s 3 November decision is the immediate domestic policy hinge.
- The BOJ held at 1.25% on 30 October. A December hike remains plausible; the relevant transmission is through USDJPY, Japanese duration and global carry.
- DXY ended near 101.9. That is above the live regime’s 101.5 disorder marker but not a decisive breakout without broader credit and breadth confirmation.

### 2. China and industrial demand

- Official October manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3; production, new orders and employment were all below 50.
- Policy rhetoric acknowledges weak domestic demand, but the October political cycle did not deliver a broad household-demand package.
- Iron ore and lithium are the clearest demand casualties. Nickel remains burdened by Indonesian supply despite quota and permitting rhetoric.
- Copper’s resilience around $6.46/lb is the exception. Treat it as a supply-quality/electrification signal until China activity and physical premiums confirm a broader cycle.

### 3. Energy and transport

- Brent ended October near $113/bbl, WTI near $103/bbl and European TTF near €103/MWh. TTF’s monthly rise was the most acute cross-asset move.
- The IEA estimates Gulf crude exports at 13.9 mb/d, about 75% of pre-war levels; refined-product and LPG flows are more impaired, and global stocks are about 509 mb below pre-war levels.
- Mid-October Gulf-to-Asia tanker costs exceeded $37/bbl, roughly five times pre-war levels. Freight, insurance and voyage duration therefore remain part of the commodity price, not noise around it.
- OPEC+ maintained September 2026 required production for December. With spare logistics and product conversion more binding than headline crude quotas, producer policy is a secondary stabiliser.

### 4. Supply constraints and structural projects

- Simandou is ramping, but Rio Tinto still targets the second half of 2028 for a 60 Mtpa run rate. It cannot offset current China weakness quickly.
- Indonesia is considering restrictions on new semi-processed nickel capacity and has reduced 2026 mining quotas, but enforcement credibility and existing scale keep nickel oversupplied.
- Uranium remains the cleanest transition commodity because supply security and contracting demand are less dependent on near-term Chinese construction.
- Central-bank gold buying and Chinese ETF demand remain structural supports, but October demonstrated that they do not prevent tactical liquidation.

## Part 2 — Asset mechanics

### Precious metals

**Gold:** Structural official-sector and Chinese ETF demand remains constructive. Tactically, the fall to about $3,876/oz despite lower real yields is a warning that positioning, dollar liquidity and forced selling can dominate. A recovery above $4,000 is needed to re-establish defensive leadership; below $3,850 keeps liquidation risk active.

**Silver:** Fell to about $48.90/oz and remains the higher-beta expression of the gold/industrial cross-current. It needs gold stabilisation and better industrial breadth before its asymmetry improves.

**Platinum and palladium:** Both weakened sharply. Automotive-cycle softness and substitution dynamics dominate near term; South African or Russian supply events are the principal upside tails.

### Battery and transition commodities

**Lithium:** Near CNY 93,600/t, down roughly one quarter in October. Supply discipline is unproven; demand growth has not yet repaired pricing power.

**Uranium:** Near $92.55/lb and up on the month. Contracting, energy security and limited primary supply support the strongest medium-term setup in this group.

**Copper:** Near $6.46/lb and broadly flat. It remains a relative-strength asset, but needs China activity, physical premiums and inventories to validate a new leg higher.

**Nickel:** Near $15,220/t. Indonesian quota discipline is the key potential regime changer, but oversupply remains the base case until realised output falls.

### Bulk commodities

**Iron ore:** Dalian near CNY 672/t and declining. China’s sub-50 PMI and property weakness outweigh longer-dated supply growth; upside needs both policy transmission and improved steel margins.

**Thermal coal:** Near $152/t, supported by global energy scarcity and gas substitution. It remains a higher-conviction inflation hedge than iron ore.

**Coking coal:** Near $268/t and modestly firmer, but benchmark comparability is weak. Use steel margins and physical Australian/Asian assessments before acting.

### Energy

**Brent/WTI:** Physical route impairment, low stocks and product scarcity keep the balance acute. A sustained Brent break above $118–120 would signal renewed escalation; a fall below $105 is not enough without flow repair.

**European TTF:** The acute marginal signal. Above €110/MWh would deepen industrial and household stress; below €90 with improved LNG/product flows would be the first meaningful relief.

**Henry Hub:** Near $2.88/mmBtu, comparatively insulated from global scarcity by US infrastructure and storage. A move above $3.20 would show spillover into domestic US gas.

### Equities

**S&P 500:** October was mildly negative, while the average stock did materially better than the cap-weighted index. That is not a classic broad liquidation.

**Nasdaq:** Fell about 5.3% in October and closed near 25,437. The trigger is no longer index weakness alone; it is index weakness plus weak breadth, wider credit and deteriorating carry.

**ASX 200:** Finished around 8,900 and gained in October. Materials and energy provide inflation exposure, but domestic duration and consumption are vulnerable to a higher RBA path.

## Part 3 — Regional policy and transmission

### United States

Growth remained firm—Q3 real GDP rose 3.4% annualised and private domestic final sales 4.9%—while September PCE inflation accelerated. The Fed therefore faces a live inflation-growth conflict rather than a recessionary easing setup. AI capex has exceeded aggregate operating cash flow across the largest platforms, making free-cash-flow quality the central equity discriminator.

### Australia

Inflation re-acceleration and an 88% market-implied probability of a November rate rise place the RBA at the centre of the local frame. The ASX’s energy/materials exposure is supportive, but higher mortgage rates and weaker household spending would hit domestic cyclicals and rate-sensitive real estate.

### China

October PMI data confirm insufficient domestic demand. Broad easing must be judged by actual household and private-sector transmission, not announcements. A credit or fiscal package without improved orders, housing activity and commodity imports is not a regime change.

### Japan

The BOJ’s slow normalisation continues. USDJPY near 157.5 is orderly but elevated. A break above 160.5 would raise intervention and imported-inflation risk; a fast move below 154 alongside rising JGB yields would mark a carry unwind.

## Part 4 — State of play

### Points of agreement

1. Acute physical-energy stress is intact and more severe than in September.
2. Inflation has re-accelerated in both the US and Australia.
3. The September duration escalation has eased, but yields remain restrictive.
4. China is not yet transmitting enough demand to bulk or battery commodities.
5. Broad disorderly risk-off is unconfirmed because breadth, credit and carry have not deteriorated synchronously.

### Dominant disagreements to monitor

- **Oil scarcity versus demand destruction:** whether high prices produce material consumption and growth damage before flows normalise.
- **AI investment versus cash-flow discipline:** whether capex produces revenue fast enough to stop multiple compression.
- **Gold structure versus tactical liquidation:** whether official buying reasserts itself above $4,000 or dollar/liquidity pressure drives another leg lower.
- **China supply discipline versus oversupply:** whether nickel and lithium announcements reduce realised production.
- **Policy resolve versus financial stability:** whether the Fed, RBA and BOJ can tighten without triggering credit or carry disorder.

### Live cross-regional transmission

`Gulf flow impairment → oil/product/freight inflation → US/Australian inflation → restrictive policy → real-income and margin compression`

`AI capex > cash generation → weaker buybacks/free cash flow → Nasdaq concentration pressure → credit/breadth confirmation test`

`China weak orders → iron ore/lithium/nickel weakness → Australian terms-of-trade drag`, partly offset by energy and copper.
